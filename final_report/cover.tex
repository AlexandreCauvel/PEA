\chapter{Introduction}
\section{Background}

Modern aerospace structures rely heavily on high-performance aluminium alloys, particularly the 2xxx and 7xxx series, due to their exceptional strength-to-weight ratio and manufacturability. However, in harsh aerospace environments, these materials are highly susceptible to localised corrosion \cite{MartinezViademonte2020,Garg2025}. This degradation is accelerated by dispersed intermetallic particles (IMPs) that form micro-galvanic sites, significantly shortening component lifespans \cite{MartinezViademonte2020,Becker2019,Garg2025}.

Traditionally, this challenge has been managed using multilayer coatings based on hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) \cite{MartinezViademonte2020,BoualiReview2020}. These coatings offered strong adhesion, long-term durability, and, most importantly, a self-healing capability \cite{MartinezViademonte2020,Becker2019,BoualiReview2020}. However, due to the carcinogenic nature of Cr(VI), strict regulatory restrictions have demanded an immediate ban, creating a need for safe, high-performance alternatives that can match the protection offered by chromium-based systems \cite{MartinezViademonte2020,Becker2019,Bouali2020}.

Recently, Layered Double Hydroxides (LDH) have emerged as a particularly promising alternative \cite{Cao2022,Becker2019,Bouali2020,Zhou2017,Iuzviuk2020,BoualiReview2020}. Their layered structure allows them to store and release corrosion inhibitors in response to changes in the environment, providing a combination of barrier protection and active self-healing that aligns well with the industry's long-term needs.

Much of the recent research on LDH coatings has focused on common wrought alloys such as AA2024, usually tested on simple flat samples \cite{Tedim2014,Tedim2011,He2020,Bouali2020}. While these studies have demonstrated excellent potential, there is a distinct lack of data on how LDH coatings perform on cast alloys like AC46000. Cast alloys make it possible to manufacture complex, highly detailed geometries that are becoming more common in modern aerospace assemblies. However, these components also introduce new challenges such as an uneven surface roughness, restricted accessibility, distinct microstructural features, and higher silicon content. All of these factors can significantly influence LDH growth, inhibitor incorporation, and overall coating uniformity, integrity and performance.

By successfully applying and testing the optimized environmentally-friendly LDH system on geometrically complex components, this work validates the limited existing research. More importantly, it establishes a basis for future investigation, especially in complex geometry which continues to grow rapidly in aerospace industry, while complying with safety and health regulations.

\section{Objectives}
This work follows four central objectives defined in the project plan:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{O1. Synthesis and Optimization:} Synthesize a Layered Double Hydroxide (LDH) conversion pre-treatment capable of forming a uniform coating layer on both flat and geometrically complex aluminium alloy substrates.
    
    \item \textbf{O2. Inhibitor Integration:} Intercalate established and novel corrosion inhibitors into the optimized LDH structure.
    
    \item \textbf{O3. Performance Benchmarking:} Compare LDH performance against established reference systems, such as anodizing and Cr(III)/Ce(IV) conversion coatings.
    
    \item \textbf{O4. Applicability to complex shapes:} Validate the protective system on complex geometric shapes, including application of an organic top-coating.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope}
The scope of this investigation encompasses the following aspects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coating System:} Development of ZnAl--LDH conversion layers on AC46000 alloy via hot aqueous synthesis at elevated temperatures, utilising partial substrate dissolution to supply aluminium cations.
    
    \item \textbf{Inhibitor Chemistry:} Screening of environmentally friendly inhibitor candidates, including sodium metavanadate, sodium molybdate, and 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (MBT), at a standard concentration of 0.005 M.
    
    \item \textbf{Physical Characterisation:} X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis to evaluate inhibitor intercalation efficiency through basal spacing measurements, and scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) to analyse coating morphology, elemental distribution, and substrate microstructure.
    
    \item \textbf{Electrochemical Assessment:} Corrosion performance evaluation through long-term electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) monitoring (up to \qty{168}{\hour} in \qty{0.05}{\molar} \ce{NaCl}) and potentiodynamic polarisation (PDP) measurements to determine corrosion kinetics and inhibition mechanisms.
    
    \item \textbf{Multilayer Configuration:} Application of a water-based polyurethane organic coating over the optimised LDH pre-treatment via anodic electrodeposition, validated on both planar and complex-geometry specimens.
\end{itemize}


\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Thermodynamics and Electrochemical Passivity}
\label{sec:thermo_passivity}

Corrosion is a spontaneous thermodynamic process driven by the tendency of metals to revert to lower-energy, oxidised states, such as oxides or sulfides \cite{Sastri2007, Revie2008, Vargel2020}. This driving force is quantified by a negative change in Gibbs free energy ($\Delta G = -nFE$), indicating that the oxidised state is energetically favoured over the metallic state for most engineering alloys \cite{Sastri2007, Ghali2010}. 

In practice, however, the electrochemical behaviour of a metal is characterised by its open-circuit potential (OCP), defined as the steady-state potential difference between the metal and a reference electrode in the absence of external current \cite{Vargel2020, Roberge2000}. For aluminium, the standard electrode potential is highly active ($E^\circ = -1.66$~V vs. SHE). Yet, the measured OCP in aqueous environments typically ranges from $-0.5$ to $-0.7$~V vs. SHE \cite{Vargel2020, Revie2008}. This noble shift is attributed to the rapid, spontaneous formation of a thin, adherent alumina ($\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$) film that passivates the surface. The stability of this passive film is highly dependent on environmental factors, particularly pH, temperature, and the concentration of aggressive anions such as chloride \cite{Vargel2020, Sastri2007, Ghali2010, Revie2008}.

\section{Thermodynamic Stability: Pourbaix Diagrams}
\label{sec:pourbaix}

Pourbaix diagrams (Figure~\ref{fig:pourbaix}) map the thermodynamic stability of a metal as a function of electrode potential and pH, delineating three primary domains: immunity (thermodynamically stable metal), corrosion (soluble ionic products), and passivation (insoluble, protective oxides or hydroxides) \cite{Revie2008, Ghali2010}. For aluminium, these diagrams confirm its amphoteric nature. Passivation is thermodynamically stable only within an intermediate pH range of approximately 4 to 8.5; outside this window, both acidic and alkaline conditions promote active dissolution \cite{Vargel2020, Ghali2010, Revie2008}. 

Crucially, Pourbaix diagrams represent equilibrium conditions and provide no insight into corrosion kinetics \cite{Revie2008, Ghali2010}. A metal residing within the passivation domain may still undergo rapid localised corrosion if the passive film is mechanically or chemically disrupted, a scenario frequently encountered in chloride-containing environments \cite{Vargel2020, Sastri2007}.

\section{Interfacial Kinetics and the Electrical Double Layer}
\label{sec:kinetics}

While thermodynamics dictates the feasibility of corrosion, the actual corrosion rate is governed by the kinetics of charge transfer at the metal--solution interface \cite{Vargel2020}. This charge exchange occurs within a highly structured interfacial region approximately 10~nm thick, known as the electrical double layer (EDL, Figure~\ref{fig:double_layer}) \cite{Vargel2020}. Upon immersion, the perturbation of local molecular and ionic arrangements creates a charge separation to maintain overall electroneutrality \cite{Vargel2020, Sastri2007}. 

The EDL is classically described by a combination of the Helmholtz, Stern, and Gouy--Chapman models, which define regions of decreasing structural order moving away from the metal surface \cite{Vargel2020, Sastri2007}. The innermost compact Stern layer contains oriented water molecules and specifically adsorbed ions. Beyond this lies the outer Helmholtz plane, marking the closest approach of solvated ions, followed by the diffuse Gouy--Chapman layer extending into the bulk electrolyte \cite{Vargel2020, Sastri2007}. The architecture of this double layer imposes physical and electrostatic barriers that directly control ionic transport and the rate of the coupled anodic and cathodic partial reactions \cite{Sastri2007}.

\section{Chloride-Induced Breakdown and Pitting Corrosion}
\label{sec:chloride_pitting}

Although the native alumina film provides effective passivation, it is highly susceptible to localised breakdown in the presence of chloride ions. Owing to their small ionic radius and low hydration energy, chloride ions readily shed their hydration shells and specifically adsorb onto the oxide surface. They compete with hydroxyl ions for adsorption sites, particularly at surface defects, grain boundaries, and intermetallic particle (IMP) interfaces \cite{Vargel2020, Zhang2018, McCafferty2010}:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Al(OH)_{ads}} + \mathrm{Cl^{-}} \rightarrow \mathrm{AlCl_{ads}} + \mathrm{OH^{-}}
    \label{eq:cl_adsorption}
\end{equation}

Once a critical local chloride concentration is achieved, the passive film ruptures. The exposed aluminium undergoes accelerated anodic dissolution, generating soluble chloride complexes. These complexes hydrolyse, producing a locally acidic environment that further accelerates dissolution and inhibits repassivation \cite{Vargel2020, Ghali2010}. This transition is defined by the pitting potential ($E_{\text{pit}}$), above which localised attack propagates spontaneously \cite{Revie2008, Ghali2010}.

Pit initiation is strongly correlated with the presence of cathodic IMPs at the alloy surface. At these specific boundaries, the aforementioned chloride adsorption locally disrupts the passive film, establishing aggressive micro-galvanic couples that drive the initial breakdown (Figure~\ref{fig:pitting}) \cite{Vargel2020, Zhang2018, Ghali2010, McCafferty2010}. Following initiation, pit growth is sustained by a well-documented autocatalytic mechanism \cite{Sastri2007, Ghali2010}. Metal dissolution generates a local excess of cations, which attracts additional chloride ions to maintain charge neutrality. The resulting metal chloride salts hydrolyse, further acidifying the pit interior. Concurrently, the surrounding cathodic regions sustain the oxygen reduction reaction, maintaining the electrochemical driving force for continued anodic dissolution \cite{Revie2008, Sastri2007, Ghali2010, Vargel2020}. 

Consequently, from a protective coating perspective, the primary objective is not merely to provide a passive barrier against electrolyte ingress. Effective protection must also incorporate active inhibition mechanisms capable of suppressing these autocatalytic processes should the barrier become locally compromised \cite{MartinezViademonte2020, BoualiReview2020}.

\section{Conventional Corrosion Protection Methods}
\label{sec:conventional}

Conventional corrosion protection systems for aluminium alloys, particularly in aerospace applications, typically employ a multilayer architecture comprising a surface pretreatment (anodic oxide or conversion coating), a corrosion-inhibiting primer, and an organic topcoat \cite{MartinezViademonte2020}. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:multilayer}, this design integrates passive barrier protection with active corrosion inhibition.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figs/multilayer.png} 
    \caption{Typical multilayer coating system for corrosion protection of 
    aluminium alloys \cite{MartinezViademonte2020}.}
    \label{fig:multilayer}
\end{figure}

Passive protection is primarily governed by the physical barrier properties of these layers, which impede electrolyte ingress. Active protection is provided by inhibitors within the primer, which mobilise upon coating damage to suppress localised corrosion at the substrate interface \cite{MartinezViademonte2020}. The foundational pretreatment layer is critical to the efficacy of this system. Anodising, an electrochemical process that grows a thick, porous aluminium oxide layer under an applied potential, offers superior corrosion and wear resistance compared to the native passive film \cite{MartinezViademonte2020}. This anodic film comprises a thin, compact barrier layer at the metal/oxide interface and a thicker, porous outer layer, the morphology of which is dictated by electrolyte composition, voltage, and treatment duration \cite{MartinezViademonte2020}. Despite these performance advantages, conversion coatings (CCs) are frequently preferred in industrial practice due to their lower processing costs, operational simplicity, and greater geometric flexibility \cite{Becker2019}.

Historically, hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)] has served as the benchmark for these protection systems, functioning both as the active component in chromate conversion coatings (CCCs) and as a soluble inhibitor in primers \cite{Beneke2016, BoualiReview2020}. Its efficacy stems from an exceptional self-healing capability, wherein soluble Cr(VI) species migrate to defect sites and are reduced to form a protective, insoluble Cr(III) oxide/hydroxide film \cite{BoualiReview2020}. However, Cr(VI) compounds are highly toxic, carcinogenic, and subject to stringent global regulatory restrictions, necessitating the development of environmentally benign alternatives \cite{MartinezViademonte2020, BoualiReview2020}.

Among the proposed replacements, trivalent chromium process (TCP) coatings have achieved a degree of industrial acceptance. Nevertheless, they exhibit diminished compatibility with modern, chromium-free primers and raise ongoing concerns regarding the potential formation of trace Cr(VI) during processing or service \cite{Becker2019, Qi2015, BoualiReview2020}. Alternatively, rare earth conversion coatings (RECCs), particularly cerium-based systems, offer reduced toxicity and performance comparable to CCCs in certain environments \cite{Becker2019, Hughes2009}. However, their widespread industrial adoption remains limited by a lack of robust self-healing mechanisms and a high sensitivity to substrate microstructure \cite{Becker2019,Sainis2020}. This is a critical drawback for heterogeneous cast aluminium alloys like AC46000, where the presence of intermetallic particles disrupts uniform coating formation.

\section{Layered Double Hydroxides as a Chromate-Free Alternative}
\label{sec:LDH}

Layered double hydroxides (LDHs) have emerged as a highly effective, chromate-free alternative for corrosion protection, directly addressing the self-healing deficit of current environmentally benign coatings. LDHs belong to a class of lamellar materials characterised by a brucite-like structure (Figure~\ref{fig:ldh_structure}). They consist of positively charged metal hydroxide host layers, charge-balanced by exchangeable anions located within the interlayer galleries \cite{Bouali2020, Zhou2017, He2020, Iuzviuk2020, Cao2022}. Their general chemical formula is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    [\mathrm{M}^{2+}_{1-x}\mathrm{M}^{3+}_{x}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}]^{x+}
    (\mathrm{A}^{n-})_{x/n} \cdot m\mathrm{H}_{2}\mathrm{O}
    \label{eq:LDH}
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{M}^{2+}$ and $\mathrm{M}^{3+}$ represent divalent and trivalent metal cations, $\mathrm{A}^{n-}$ denotes the $n$-valent interlayer anions, $x$ is the molar ratio $\mathrm{M}^{3+}/(\mathrm{M}^{2+} + \mathrm{M}^{3+})$ (typically 0.2--0.33), and $m$ represents the number of interlayer water molecules \cite{He2020, Cao2022}. The cationic composition is highly tuneable, provided the ionic radii are compatible with octahedral coordination within the hydroxide layer \cite{He2020}. 

Fundamentally, the relatively weak electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions between the host layers and the interlayer anions confer LDHs with their most technologically relevant property: facile anion exchange. This allows inhibiting anions, pre-loaded into the interlayer galleries, to be exchanged for aggressive species (such as chloride) upon electrolyte ingress \cite{Bouali2020, BoualiReview2020} (Figure~\ref{fig:ldh_mechanism}). This mechanism provides active, self-healing corrosion inhibition analogous to chromate-based systems, but without the associated toxicity. Consequently, LDHs have been successfully demonstrated on a variety of substrates, including aluminium, zinc, and magnesium alloys, as well as anodised surfaces and galvanised steels \cite{Iuzviuk2020, Buchheit2003}.

\subsection{Dual-Action Protection Mechanism of LDHs}
\label{subsec:LDH_advantages}

The corrosion protection efficacy of LDHs stems from a synergistic, dual-action mechanism that can be exploited in various coating architectures \cite{Iuzviuk2020, He2020, Cao2022, Zhou2017, Bouali2020}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scavenging of aggressive species:} LDHs actively trap harmful anions---most notably chloride---within their interlayer galleries. By sequestering these corrosive species, LDHs reduce the local electrolyte aggressiveness at the metal/coating interface, thereby delaying pit initiation.
    
    \item \textbf{Stimuli-responsive release of inhibitors:} Concurrently, the exchange process releases pre-loaded inhibiting anions (e.g., vanadate, molybdate, or organic inhibitors) into the local environment. This release is often triggered by local pH changes or the presence of aggressive anions, delivering targeted self-healing protection precisely where the coating is compromised.
\end{enumerate}

Beyond this primary ion-exchange behaviour, LDHs exhibit a secondary self-healing capability through local dissolution and recrystallisation at coating defects. This process can physically restore barrier integrity and seal micro-cracks \cite{Cao2022}. This multifaceted protection is particularly advantageous for heterogeneous microstructures, such as those found in AC46000 cast alloys, where localised galvanic activity demands robust, targeted inhibition.

\subsection{Synthesis of LDH}
\label{subsec:LDH_synthesis}

Layered double hydroxides can be deployed in corrosion protection systems either \textit{ex situ} as pre-synthesised powders or \textit{in situ} as films grown directly on the metal substrate. \textit{Ex situ} synthesis is predominantly achieved via co-precipitation under constant or variable pH conditions \cite{BoualiReview2020}. The resulting powders are typically incorporated as active pigments within single-layer sol--gel hybrid coatings \cite{Yasakau2014, Alvarez2010} or dispersed throughout multilayer architectures, a strategy frequently adopted in industrial sectors such as automotive manufacturing \cite{BoualiReview2020}.

Despite this versatility, \textit{in situ} LDH growth is generally preferred for corrosion protection applications. Direct growth promotes the formation of chemical bonds between the LDH film and the substrate, yielding superior interfacial adhesion \cite{BoualiReview2020, Cao2022, Guo2009}. Furthermore, \textit{in situ} film formation is a single-step process largely independent of substrate geometry, rendering it highly suitable for complex or large-area components \cite{Guo2009, BoualiReview2020}.

The most widely adopted \textit{in situ} methodologies for growing LDHs on aluminium are hydrothermal synthesis and \textit{in situ} co-precipitation. While conventional co-precipitation typically relies on the mixing of external divalent and trivalent metal salt solutions \cite{BoualiReview2020}, \textit{in situ} approaches offer a distinct advantage by utilising the aluminium substrate itself as the trivalent ($\mathrm{Al}^{3+}$) precursor. 

True hydrothermal synthesis requires sealed autoclaves to achieve the elevated temperatures and autogenous pressures necessary for crystallisation \cite{Guo2009}. However, an alternative \textit{in situ} route, frequently documented in recent literature \cite{Bouali2021}, employs a hot aqueous solution method under atmospheric pressure. In this approach, the substrate is immersed in a solution containing the requisite divalent cations (e.g., $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$) at near-boiling temperatures (typically 95~$^\circ$C). The controlled, partial dissolution of the aluminium substrate continuously supplies $\mathrm{Al}^{3+}$ ions to the interface, driving the local precipitation of the LDH phase. This atmospheric \textit{in situ} method not only ensures robust chemical bonding between the film and the substrate, but also offers significant practical advantages over hydrothermal treatments, including scalability, lower energy demands, and compatibility with complex geometries \cite{Cao2022,Bouali2021}.

Alternative \textit{in situ} routes, such as electrodeposition and steam coating, have also been explored. While electrodeposition offers reduced processing times, it frequently yields films with inferior adhesion \cite{He2020, Cao2022}. Conversely, steam coating provides strong interfacial bonding through an environmentally benign, solvent-free process \cite{Pan2023, Cao2022}.

Structural characterisation of the resulting LDH films is predominantly performed using X-ray diffraction (XRD). For LDH materials, the low-angle (003) and (006) basal reflections are the definitive signatures of the layered structure. By applying Bragg's law ($n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$), the $d_{003}$ interplanar spacing can be calculated directly. This parameter serves as a critical diagnostic metric: it confirms the successful formation of the LDH phase and, crucially, verifies the successful intercalation of inhibitor anions through observable shifts in the $2\theta$ peak position \cite{Zhou2017, Cao2022}.

\subsection{Rationale for ZnAl--LDH on Aluminium Substrates}
\label{subsec:ZnAl}

Among the diverse range of possible LDH compositions, ZnAl--LDH has emerged as the preeminent system for the \textit{in situ} corrosion protection of aluminium alloys. This preference is grounded in both thermodynamic compatibility and processing kinetics. 

As outlined in Section~\ref{subsec:LDH_synthesis}, the \textit{in situ} hot aqueous solution method on aluminium relies on the controlled dissolution of the substrate to supply the requisite trivalent $\mathrm{Al}^{3+}$ cations, while the divalent cation is introduced externally via the synthesis solution \cite{BoualiReview2020, Cao2022}. Of the candidate divalent cations, $\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}$ is particularly advantageous. Its ionic radius is highly compatible with the octahedral coordination required within the brucite-like host layer. Furthermore, Zn-based LDH systems consistently exhibit rapid anion exchange kinetics and robust, stimuli-responsive inhibitor release in chloride-containing environments \cite{Bouali2020, Tedim2011, BoualiReview2020, Cao2022}.

Consequently, the combination of well-established synthesis protocols, predictable inhibitor intercalation behaviour, and proven efficacy on aluminium substrates establishes ZnAl--LDH as the optimal model system for this study. However, the ultimate protective performance of this architecture is fundamentally governed by the specific choice of the intercalated inhibitor anion, which is examined in the following subsection.

\subsection{Corrosion Inhibitors}
\label{subsec:inhibitors}

LDHs are typically synthesised with nitrate ions as the initial interlayer anions, owing to their weak affinity for the LDH structure and consequently high exchangeability. Carbonate anions, by contrast, exhibit a significantly stronger affinity and resist subsequent exchange, rendering them unsuitable as precursor anions \cite{Cao2022}. Once the nitrate-LDH is synthesised, it undergoes an anion-exchange step to intercalate the desired corrosion-inhibiting species. 

The nature of the inhibitor dictates the intercalation strategy. Unlike inorganic inhibitors such as vanadate, thiol-containing organic inhibitors require alkaline pH adjustment prior to intercalation. While 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (MBT) requires this adjustment to achieve sufficient aqueous solubility, L-cysteine is already highly soluble at neutral pH as a zwitterion. Nevertheless, for both molecules, adjusting the solution pH with \ce{NaOH} is essential to deprotonate the thiol group (\ce{-SH}) into its thiolate anion (\ce{-S^-}). This deprotonation imparts the net negative charge required for electrostatic uptake into the positively charged LDH galleries \cite{Tedim2010}.

Vanadates are among the most extensively studied inhibitor systems, with active corrosion protection of AA2024 demonstrated by \citet{Tedim2011} and \citet{Yasakau2014}. \citet{Zhang2015} further showed that vanadate-intercalated ZnAl--LDH provides superior corrosion resistance relative to nitrate-intercalated LDH on the same alloy, while \citet{Zhou2017} reported analogous protective behaviour for AZ91 magnesium alloy. Other inhibitors, including MBT and molybdate, have also been investigated \cite{Cao2022, Tedim2010, Zhang2015}; however, for AA2024, these were found to offer inferior protection relative to vanadates \cite{Zhang2015, Tedim2010}. Crucially, the relative performance of these inhibitors is strongly dependent on both the LDH composition and the substrate chemistry. For instance, \citet{Matsui2025} reported that molybdate-intercalated Mg--Al LDH provided superior corrosion resistance compared to vanadate-based systems on ADC-12, an Al--Si--Cu die-cast alloy. This underscores the critical importance of tailoring inhibitor selection to the specific microstructural and electrochemical characteristics of the alloy system under investigation.

\section{Influence of Microstructural Heterogeneity on LDH Growth}
\label{sec:imp_ldh}

AC46000 (equivalent to the A380 designation) is a hypoeutectic Al--Si--Cu die-cast alloy widely utilised in aerospace and automotive applications due to its excellent castability, favourable mechanical properties, and cost-effectiveness \cite{Kasinska2020}. Its nominal composition includes approximately 8--11\,wt.\,\% Si, 2--4\,wt.\,\% Cu, and minor quantities of Fe, Mn, Mg, and Zn \cite{Kasinska2020, Gudic2024}. The rapid solidification inherent to the die-casting process results in a highly heterogeneous microstructure comprising an aluminium matrix, a eutectic Si phase, and a complex network of intermetallic particles (IMPs). The principal IMP phases in AC46000 include copper-rich phases (e.g., \ce{Al2Cu}, \ce{Al7Cu2Fe}) and iron-rich phases (e.g., \ce{Al5FeSi}, \ce{Al15(Fe,Mn)3Si2}) \cite{Gudic2024}. 

This microstructural heterogeneity introduces significant electrochemical heterogeneity. When exposed to an electrolyte, micro-galvanic couples form between the noble IMPs (acting as cathodes) and the less noble aluminium matrix (acting as the anode), driving preferential anodic dissolution \cite{Vargel2020, Gudic2024}. As discussed below, this galvanic activity not only dictates the corrosion behaviour of the bare alloy but also fundamentally governs the spatial distribution and homogeneity of \textit{in situ} LDH film growth.

Copper-rich phases are strongly cathodic relative to the Al matrix, with \ce{Al2Cu} exhibiting a potential difference of approximately +400~mV \cite{Gudic2024}. This drives intense localised galvanic activity, promoting rapid oxygen reduction and a sharp local increase in pH. During \textit{in situ} LDH synthesis, these alkaline micro-environments trigger the preferential, rapid precipitation of thick, ``flower-like'' LDH islands directly over the cathodic sites \cite{Cao2026} (Figure~\ref{fig:ldh_nucleation}). 

However, this localised growth is sustained by the aggressive anodic dissolution of the surrounding aluminium matrix, a phenomenon known as ``trenching'' \cite{Bouali2022}. Trenching generates voids at the periphery of the intermetallic particles where LDH nucleation is inhibited \cite{Bouali2022}. These uncoated gaps represent critical defects, providing direct pathways for chloride ingress and pit initiation. Furthermore, this inhomogeneous film morphology compromises the adhesion of subsequently applied polymer topcoats and, in severe cases, can lead to the mechanical detachment of the IMPs, entirely undermining the integrity of the protective system \cite{Bouali2022}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{figs/ldh_nucleation.png} 
    \caption{Generalised mechanism of LDH nucleation and matrix trenching at a Cu-rich intermetallic compound in an aluminium alloy \cite{Bouali2022}.}
    \label{fig:ldh_nucleation}
\end{figure}

Similarly, iron-rich phases act as strong cathodes ($\approx +300$~mV relative to the matrix), exacerbating this inhomogeneous film growth and increasing localised corrosion susceptibility \cite{Gudic2024}. 

Conversely, the eutectic silicon particles present a distinctly different growth scenario. Although they are cathodic relative to the aluminium matrix ($\approx +250$~mV), they are electrochemically inert and do not supply the requisite $\ce{Al^{3+}}$ cations through anodic dissolution \cite{Gudic2024, DelOlmo2025}. Consequently, they do not trigger the intense, localised alkaline conditions that drive rapid LDH nucleation. Nevertheless, experimental evidence demonstrates that LDH does indeed form over these eutectic Si phases. This growth is attributed not to localised galvanic activation, but rather to general precipitation driven by the supersaturation of LDH precursors supplied by the dissolving surrounding matrix. Although this provides surface coverage, the morphology and density of the LDH formed over the inert Si particles typically differ from the robust films formed over the active intermetallics. 

Ultimately, the inherent microstructural heterogeneity of AC46000 dictates a highly non-uniform LDH growth profile. Navigating these distinct, competing growth mechanisms across the various microstructural constituents presents a significant challenge that must be addressed to ensure consistent, high-quality corrosion protection.

\subsection{Pre-Treatment Protocols}
\label{subsec:pre_treatment}

The removal of surface oxides and intermetallic particles (IMPs) prior to LDH synthesis is essential to ensure uniform film nucleation and strong interfacial adhesion. This is typically achieved through a sequential, multi-step pre-treatment protocol comprising degreasing, alkaline etching, and acid desmutting (pickling) \cite{MartinezViademonte2020}.

Degreasing constitutes the initial step, aimed at eliminating surface contaminants such as machining oils and lubricants. This ensures uniform surface wettability, which is a strict prerequisite for consistent chemical interaction during all subsequent processing stages \cite{MartinezViademonte2020}. 

The subsequent alkaline etching step serves a dual purpose: dissolving the native, non-uniform oxide layer and removing surface-exposed IMPs. During this stage, the cathodic IMPs act as active sites for the hydrogen evolution or oxygen reduction reactions, generating a highly localised alkaline microenvironment. This accelerates the anodic dissolution of the surrounding aluminium matrix, progressively undermining the mechanical anchoring of the particles. As the matrix recedes, scallop-shaped voids form, eventually leading to the detachment of the IMPs---a process commonly referred to as cathodic undercutting \cite{Nelson2001, Jin2020}. Because many of the alloying elements (e.g., Cu, Fe) released during this dissolution are insoluble in alkaline media, they redeposit on the surface as a loosely adherent, dark residue known as ``smut'' \cite{MartinezViademonte2020, Nelson2001, Jin2020}.

The removal of this smut necessitates an acid desmutting step. Nitric acid (\ce{HNO3}) is widely employed for this purpose, as it acts as a strong oxidant that effectively dissolves metallic smut residues while simultaneously repassivating the exposed aluminium matrix \cite{Jin2020}. However, for high-silicon alloys like AC46000, nitric acid alone is entirely ineffective against the eutectic silicon phases \cite{ASM1994}. To address this critical limitation, fluoride-containing compounds (such as hydrofluoric acid or ammonium bifluoride) must be incorporated into the pickling bath \cite{Askin1995, Lee2008}. Fluoride ions actively complex with and dissolve silica remnants, ensuring a completely clean, uniformly reactive aluminium surface that is optimally prepared for homogeneous LDH nucleation \cite{Askin1995, Lee2008}.

\section{Electrochemical Characterisation Techniques}
\label{sec:electrochemical_techniques}

\subsection{Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy}
\label{subsec:EIS}

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) is a cornerstone non-destructive technique for evaluating the corrosion protection performance of coated metal systems. By applying a small-amplitude sinusoidal potential perturbation (typically 5--15~mV) over a broad frequency range, EIS measures the system's complex impedance response without significantly disturbing its electrochemical steady state \cite{Orazem2008, Barsoukov2005, Lasia2014}.

The resulting complex impedance data, $Z(\omega) = Z' + jZ''$, are typically analysed in two complementary formats. The Nyquist plot ($-Z''$ vs. $Z'$) is highly effective for visualising distinct electrochemical processes, while the Bode plot (impedance modulus $|Z|$ and phase angle $\phi$ vs. frequency on a logarithmic scale) provides superior resolution for identifying overlapping time constants \cite{Orazem2008, Lasia2014}. 

\subsection{Potentiodynamic Polarisation}
Potentiodynamic polarisation (PDP) monitors shifts in the corrosion potential ($E_{\text{corr}}$) and enables qualitative comparison of anodic and cathodic behaviour. Complementing EIS, PDP reveals how the LDH coating modifies fundamental reaction pathways without requiring kinetic parameter extraction.

\section{Equivalent Circuit Models for EIS}
\label{sec:ecm}

Determining an appropriate equivalent circuit model (ECM) is a critical step in EIS analysis, as it enables the deconvolution of overlapping electrochemical processes and their quantification through physically meaningful parameters. The selection of the circuit topology must reflect the actual physicochemical processes occurring at the electrode/electrolyte interface. One of the most fundamental models is the Randles equivalent circuit (REC), which describes a simple electrochemical reaction controlled by charge transfer kinetics (Figure~\ref{fig:randles}) \cite{Das2025, Lasia2014}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.25\textwidth]{figs/randles.png} 
    \caption{Randles equivalent circuit.}
    \label{fig:randles}
\end{figure}

The REC comprises three primary elements, each corresponding to a specific physical phenomenon:

\paragraph{Solution Resistance ($R_s$)}
The solution resistance quantifies the ohmic resistive potential drop between the working and reference electrodes \cite{MetrohmEIS3, Lasia2014}. Its magnitude is dictated by the electrolyte conductivity and the cell geometry. In a Nyquist representation, $R_s$ is identified as the high-frequency intercept of the impedance data with the real axis, representing the purely resistive contribution of the bulk electrolyte before any interfacial charge transfer or capacitive processes occur.

\paragraph{Charge-Transfer Resistance ($R_{ct}$)}
The charge-transfer resistance represents the kinetic resistance to electron transfer across the electrode/electrolyte interface. As a strictly kinetic parameter, it is inversely proportional to the rate of the Faradaic reactions. In corrosion studies, $R_{ct}$ is often equated to the polarisation resistance ($R_p$), which relates directly to the corrosion current density ($i_{\text{corr}}$) via the Stern--Geary relationship \cite{Kelly2002, Lasia2014}:

\begin{equation}
    i_{\text{corr}} = \frac{B}{R_{\text{ct}}} \label{eq:icorr}
\end{equation}

where $B$ is the Stern--Geary constant. Consequently, a higher $R_{ct}$ reflects slower interfacial kinetics and a lower corrosion rate, indicating superior corrosion protection. In the Nyquist plot, $R_{ct}$ manifests as the diameter of the capacitive semicircle in the high-to-mid frequency range, arising from the kinetic time constant $\tau_{k} = R_{ct} C_{dl}$ \cite{Das2025, Lasia2014}.

\paragraph{Double-Layer Capacitance ($C_{dl}$) and the Constant Phase Element (CPE)}
The double-layer capacitance arises from the charge separation at the electrode/electrolyte interface, as described in Section~\ref{sec:kinetics} \cite{Kelly2002, MetrohmEIS3}. While ideal capacitors assume a perfectly smooth, homogeneous surface, solid electrodes---particularly heterogeneous cast alloys and rough conversion coatings---invariably exhibit frequency dispersion due to surface roughness, porosity, and non-uniform current distribution \cite{Lasia2014, Das2025}. 

To account for this non-ideal behaviour, the double layer is most accurately represented by a Constant Phase Element (CPE). The impedance of a CPE is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{CPE}} = \frac{1}{Q(j\omega)^n} \label{eq:cpe}
\end{equation}

where $Q$ is the CPE constant, $\omega$ is angular frequency, and $n$ is the dispersion coefficient ($0 \le n \le 1$). For coated aluminium systems, $n$ typically ranges from 0.8 to 0.95, where deviations from unity quantitatively reflect surface heterogeneity and coating degradation \cite{Lasia2014, Das2025}.

From a corrosion monitoring perspective, the effective capacitance of the interface is a highly sensitive indicator of coating integrity. For an LDH-protected surface, the measured capacitance comprises contributions from both the LDH film itself and the underlying electrical double layer. As the protective film degrades or becomes increasingly porous, the electrolyte penetrates the coating. This ingress increases the effective permittivity of the interfacial medium and exposes a larger effective area of the bare metal substrate. According to the planar capacitor model:

\begin{equation}
    C = \frac{\epsilon \epsilon_0 A}{d} \label{eq:capacitance}
\end{equation}

where $\epsilon$ is the relative permittivity, $\epsilon_0$ is the vacuum permittivity, $A$ is the effective interfacial area, and $d$ is the dielectric thickness. Consequently, progressive coating breakdown and the onset of localised pitting drive the measured capacitance proportionally higher, often preceding any macroscopic visual evidence of corrosion \cite{Kelly2002, Lasia2014}.


\subsection{Advanced Equivalent Circuit Models}
\label{subsec:advanced_circuits}

While the Randles circuit adequately describes a bare metal undergoing a single-step charge-transfer reaction, real-world protective systems---such as porous LDH coatings on heterogeneous alloys---exhibit multiple overlapping time constants that cannot be captured by a single RC network. To model these complex responses, advanced equivalent circuits are constructed by combining multiple resistor--capacitor (RC) or resistor--constant phase element (R-CPE) sub-networks.

When a system displays two distinct time constants, typically manifesting as two partially or fully resolved semicircles in the Nyquist plot, the two most common topologies are the series (Voigt-type) configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:voigt}) and the nested (ladder) configuration (Figure~\ref{fig:nested}). In the series configuration, each RC sub-network represents a physically distinct and sequential process \cite{Lasia2014, Kelly2002}. Conversely, the nested configuration is the standard physical representation for defective or porous protective coatings, where simultaneous and spatially overlapping processes occur \cite{Kelly2002}. In this topology, the outer RC network represents the macroscopic response of the porous coating, characterised by the film capacitance ($C_{\text{film}}$) and film resistance ($R_{\text{film}}$). The inner, nested RC network describes the actively corroding metal/electrolyte interface at the base of the pores, represented by the double-layer capacitance ($C_{\text{dl}}$) and charge-transfer resistance ($R_{\text{ct}}$) \cite{Kelly2002}. It is crucial to note that while a series Voigt circuit and a nested ladder circuit can be mathematically equivalent and produce identical impedance spectra \cite{Lasia2014}, the selection of the nested topology must be justified by the physical reality of the porous coating system.

   \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.48\textwidth}
       \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/2RC_voigt.pdf}
       \caption{Series configuration (Voigt) of a 2RC system.}
       \label{fig:voigt}
   \end{subfigure}
   \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.48\textwidth}
       \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/2RC_nested.pdf}
       \caption{Nested configuration of a 2RC system.}
       \label{fig:nested}
   \end{subfigure}
   \caption{Different configurations for 2RC systems.}
   \label{fig:2RC}
\end{figure}

The film resistance, $R_{\text{film}}$, serves a similar diagnostic function to $R_{\text{ct}}$, as both manifest as semicircle diameters in the Nyquist plot and inversely reflect the degree of protection at their respective interfaces. However, a critical distinction exists: while $R_{\text{ct}}$ is a kinetic parameter governing electron transfer, $R_{\text{film}}$ is a transport parameter. It represents the resistance to ionic current flow through the bulk of the coating and any microscopic pores connecting the electrolyte to the metal surface \cite{Kelly2002}. Consequently, a high $R_{\text{film}}$ indicates restricted ionic transport and is directly associated with superior barrier protection \cite{Lasia2014, Kelly2002}.

Similarly, $C_{\text{film}}$ acts as a sensitive, early indicator of coating degradation. For porous inorganic coatings like LDHs, the evolution of $C_{\text{film}}$ is governed primarily by electrolyte penetration. Upon immersion, the aqueous electrolyte progressively fills the micro-pores and interlayer galleries of the LDH. Given the substantially higher dielectric constant of water ($\varepsilon_r \approx 80$) relative to the dry coating matrix, this ingress causes a measurable increase in $C_{\text{film}}$ \cite{MetrohmEIS3, Lasia2014, Kelly2002}. A progressive rise in this parameter over time is therefore indicative of ongoing pore saturation, coating degradation, and a reduction in overall protective performance.

\paragraph{Warburg Impedance and Diffusion-Controlled Processes}
In electrochemical systems where the rate of the interfacial reaction exceeds the rate at which reactants can be supplied or products removed, mass transport becomes the rate-limiting step \cite{Orazem2008, Barsoukov2005}. Under these conditions, a concentration gradient develops near the electrode surface, and the resulting diffusion impedance manifests in the Nyquist plot as a characteristic $45^\circ$ linear tail at low frequencies, known as the Warburg impedance \cite{Orazem2008}. In the context of LDH-protected aluminium alloys, this behaviour typically arises at extended immersion times. It reflects the diffusion-controlled transport of aggressive species (e.g., $\mathrm{Cl}^{-}$) through the porous LDH layer, or conversely, the diffusion-limited release of intercalated inhibitors from the LDH galleries to the active corrosion site \cite{Orazem2008, Huang2018}. In bounded systems where diffusion is confined to a finite-thickness layer, this contribution is accurately represented by a finite-length Warburg element \cite{Barsoukov2005, Huang2018}.

\subsection{Quantifying Inhibitor Efficiency via EIS Parameters}
\label{subsec:inhibitor_efficiency}

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy provides a non-destructive means of quantifying the protective performance of corrosion inhibitors through the analysis of equivalent circuit parameters extracted from the impedance response \cite{Orazem2008, Lamaka2007}. The most direct indicator of inhibitor efficiency is the charge-transfer resistance, $R_{\text{ct}}$. Because it is inversely proportional to the corrosion current density (Equation~\ref{eq:icorr}), an increase in $R_{\text{ct}}$ following inhibitor addition directly reflects a suppression of the anodic dissolution or cathodic reduction kinetics at active sites \cite{Revie2008, Orazem2008}. The inhibitor efficiency, $\eta$, can therefore be expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    \eta = \left(1 - \frac{R_{\text{ct},0}}{R_{\text{ct},i}}\right) \times 100\,\%
    \label{eq:IE}
\end{equation}

where $R_{\text{ct},0}$ and $R_{\text{ct},i}$ are the charge-transfer resistances measured in the absence and presence of the inhibitor, respectively \cite{Lamaka2007, Orazem2008}. 

Beyond $R_{\text{ct}}$, the interfacial capacitance ($C_{\text{dl}}$ or $C_{\text{film}}$) provides complementary mechanistic insights. A decrease in capacitance upon inhibitor exposure typically indicates the adsorption of inhibitor species at the metal surface, which displaces water molecules and reduces the local dielectric permittivity \cite{Sastri2007, Orazem2008}. In systems utilising LDH coatings as active inhibitor reservoirs, the temporal evolution of both $R_{\text{film}}$ and $R_{\text{ct}}$ offers a definitive electrochemical signature of self-healing behaviour. Specifically, an initial decline in $R_{\text{film}}$ due to electrolyte ingress, followed by the stabilisation or recovery of $R_{\text{ct}}$, confirms that active inhibitor release is successfully compensating for localised barrier degradation \cite{BoualiReview2020, MartinezViademonte2020, Tedim2010}.

\section{Equivalent Circuit Modelling and Automated Fitting Strategy}
\label{sec:fitting_software}

Determining an appropriate equivalent circuit model (ECM) is critical for deconvoluting overlapping electrochemical processes and quantifying physically meaningful parameters, such as charge-transfer resistance ($R_{\text{ct}}$) and double layer capacitance ($C_{\text{dl}}$). However, ECM identification remains a laborious task. Commercial software packages (e.g., ZView, Gamry Echem Analyst) rely on nonlinear least-squares (NLLS) fitting, typically utilising the Levenberg--Marquardt (LM) algorithm. These routines are highly sensitive to initial parameter estimates and frequently converge to local minima, requiring considerable operator experience to yield physically reasonable solutions \cite{GamryEISModeling, Zou2023}.

To automate this process, several open-source Python frameworks have emerged. While tools such as \texttt{AutoECM} \cite{Schaeffer2023} and \texttt{AutoEIS} \cite{Zhang2023} incorporate machine learning and Bayesian inference, both suffer from dependency obsolescence, rendering them incompatible with current computational environments. Consequently, \texttt{impedance.py} \cite{Murbach2020} remains the most viable open-source option. In its standard implementation, it requires the user to supply an \textit{a priori} circuit topology and initial parameter guesses. Although it offers a basin-hopping global optimiser (\texttt{global\_opt}), this feature is fundamentally incompatible with modulus weighting (\texttt{weight\_by\_modulus}). Given that EIS datasets span several decades in both frequency and impedance magnitude, modulus weighting is essential to prevent low-frequency, high-impedance data from disproportionately dominating the fit residuals.

To overcome the reliance on manual initial guesses while retaining the statistical rigour of local optimisation, a custom hybrid fitting framework was developed, building upon \texttt{impedance.py} and the Differential Evolution (DE) algorithm. DE is a stochastic, population-based global optimisation metaheuristic that requires no gradient information, making it highly robust against the complex, multimodal parameter landscapes characteristic of multi-element ECMs \cite{Ahmad2022, Storn1997, Leite2022}.

In the proposed workflow, the user supplies only physically motivated parameter bounds for each circuit element. To accommodate the vast scale disparity between components (e.g., kilohm resistances versus microfarad capacitances), the DE optimisation is performed in logarithmic space, ensuring each order of magnitude is weighted uniformly. Once the DE stage yields a robust global estimate, the parameter vector is refined using a Nelder--Mead simplex algorithm to navigate local roughness without requiring derivatives. Finally, this refined vector serves as the initial condition for the Levenberg--Marquardt local optimiser within \texttt{impedance.py}, with \texttt{weight\_by\_modulus} activated. This final stage yields statistically meaningful parameter estimates alongside their associated confidence intervals.

\section{Surface Pre-treatments}
\label{sec:pretreatments}

As established in the literature review, the heterogeneous distribution of intermetallic particles (IMPs) on the AC46000 surface severely disrupts the homogeneity of \textit{in situ} LDH growth. To mitigate this, the native oxide and surface-exposed IMPs must be removed prior to coating deposition. In this study, three distinct surface preparation routes were evaluated alongside a reference as-polished condition. To assess the efficacy of each protocol in removing IMPs and preparing the surface for uniform LDH nucleation, the treated morphologies and residual elemental compositions were characterised using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

\subsection{Chemical Pre-treatment}
\label{subsec:chemical_pretreatment}

The simplified chemical pre-treatment comprised a two-step sequence: alkaline etching in a \qty{0.1}{\molar} \ce{NaOH} solution at room temperature for \qty{120}{\second}, followed by acid desmutting in a \qty{0.1}{\molar} \ce{HNO3} solution for \qty{480}{\second}. Each chemical step was followed by a thorough rinse with deionised water, and the specimens were ultimately dried under a stream of ambient air.

\subsection{Industrial Pre-treatment}
\label{subsec:industrial_pretreatment}

The industrial pre-treatment followed a standard commercial multistep protocol. Degreasing was performed in Metaclean T2001 (an alkaline cleaner based on metal silicates, complexing agents, and surfactants) at a concentration of \qty{48}{\gram\per\liter} and \qty{65}{\degreeCelsius} for \qty{10}{\minute}. Although the manufacturer recommends a \qty{25}{\minute} immersion, a shorter duration was deemed sufficient as no machining oils were introduced during specimen preparation. 

Following a deionised water rinse, alkaline etching was conducted in Turco Liquid Aluminetch N2 (a sodium hydroxide and phosphate-based etchant) at \qty{38}{\gram\per\liter} and \qty{65}{\degreeCelsius} for \qty{45}{\second}. Subsequently, desmutting was carried out in Turco Liquid Smutgo NC (a solution containing ferric sulfate, nitric acid, sodium bifluoride, and sulfuric acid) at a concentration of \qty{180}{\milli\liter\per\liter} and \qty{30}{\degreeCelsius} for approximately \qty{5}{\minute}. The specimens were then finally rinsed with deionised water and air-dried.

\subsection{Anodising}
\label{subsec:anodising}

For a subset of specimens, a tartaric-sulphuric acid (TSA) anodising step was integrated immediately following the industrial pre-treatment and prior to LDH synthesis. The anodising bath comprised \qty{80}{\gram\per\liter} tartaric acid and \qty{42}{\gram\per\liter} sulphuric acid, maintained at \qty{39}{\degreeCelsius}. An AA1050 aluminium plate served as the counter electrode in a two-electrode configuration. A constant voltage of \qty{14}{\volt} was applied for \qty{20}{\minute}. Following the anodising step, the specimens were thoroughly rinsed with deionised water and air-dried before undergoing LDH synthesis.

\section{LDH Synthesis}
\label{sec:LDH_synthesis}

To systematically evaluate the influence of surface pre-treatment severity and synthesis duration on LDH formation, an experimental matrix was established comprising distinct sample groups. The primary conditions investigated were an industrial pre-treatment coupled with a \qty{1}{\hour} synthesis (hereafter designated as \textbf{LDH-1}), and a simplified chemical pre-treatment coupled with a \qty{3}{\hour} synthesis (designated as \textbf{LDH-2}). 

Furthermore, to investigate whether the introduction of a porous anodic oxide layer could mitigate the inherent microstructural heterogeneity of the AC46000 alloy and promote more uniform LDH nucleation, an additional subset of specimens from both the industrial and chemical pre-treatment groups underwent a tartaric-sulphuric acid (TSA) anodising step prior to LDH synthesis. Crucially, to isolate the specific influence of the anodic layer from the variable of synthesis duration, all anodised specimens were subjected to a fixed LDH growth time of \qty{1}{\hour}, regardless of their pre-treatment route. Aside from this, the identical \textit{in situ} hot aqueous solution synthesis procedure was employed for all specimens to ensure strict comparability.

The synthesis solution was prepared by dissolving ammonium nitrate (\ce{NH4NO3}, \qty{0.6}{\molar} final concentration) in \qty{600}{\milli\liter} of deionised water (resistivity $\geq$ \qty{18}{\mega\ohm\centi\meter}) under continuous magnetic stirring. Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (\ce{Zn(NO3)2.6H2O}, \qty{0.1}{\molar} final concentration) was then added, along with an additional \qty{300}{\milli\liter} of deionised water. The solution pH was adjusted to $6.5 \pm 0.1$ using a 5\% aqueous ammonia solution. Finally, deionised water was added to bring the total volume to \qty{1}{\liter}, and the pH was verified and readjusted to $6.5 \pm 0.1$ if necessary.

For the LDH film growth, \qty{300}{\milli\liter} of the prepared synthesis solution was transferred to a glass vessel and pre-heated to \qty{95}{\degreeCelsius} on a hotplate under atmospheric pressure. This volume was sufficient to fully immerse three specimens simultaneously. The specimens were suspended vertically in the solution under continuous magnetic stirring for their respective durations (\qty{1}{\hour} or \qty{3}{\hour}). 

Upon completion of the synthesis, the specimens were immediately removed, thoroughly rinsed with deionised water to halt the reaction, and dried under a stream of compressed air. To characterise the resulting LDH films, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted. From the diffraction patterns, the basal spacing ($d_{003}$) was calculated using Bragg's law, providing definitive structural confirmation of the LDH phase and enabling a direct comparison of film crystallinity across the various pre-treatment, duration, and anodising conditions.

\section{Inhibitor Incorporation}
\label{sec:inhibitor_incorporation}

Following the electrochemical pre-selection study (Section~\ref{sec:inhibitor_selection}), the most effective inhibitor systems were selected for intercalation into the LDH interlayer galleries. The specific selection criteria and comparative results are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:results_inhibitor_selection}. The intercalation procedures for each selected inhibitor are described below.

To incorporate vanadate, anion exchange was achieved by immersing the as-grown LDH films in a \qty{0.1}{\molar} sodium metavanadate (\ce{NaVO3}) solution at \qty{25}{\degreeCelsius} for \qty{3}{\hour}. Following the exchange, the specimens were thoroughly rinsed with deionised water and dried under a stream of compressed air.

The incorporation of 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (MBT) required a modified approach due to its low aqueous solubility in its neutral state. To facilitate intercalation, the molecule must be deprotonated into its anionic form (\ce{MBT^-}). To achieve this, sodium hydroxide was added at a concentration of \qty{0.1}{\molar} to a \qty{0.1}{\molar} MBT solution. Given the amphoteric nature of aluminium and its susceptibility to alkaline dissolution (Section~\ref{sec:pourbaix}), the final solution pH was carefully adjusted to 10.2 using a \qty{1}{\molar} \ce{NaOH} titrant. This specific pH was selected to ensure sufficient deprotonation for solubility and electrostatic uptake, while remaining low enough to mitigate substrate attack. To further minimise the risk of surface degradation, the immersion time was strictly limited to \qty{30}{\minute}, with visual inspections conducted at \qty{10}{\minute} intervals. Upon completion, the specimens were immediately rinsed with deionised water and dried under a stream of compressed air.

A chemically tailored approach was adopted for L-cysteine, which also requires deprotonation to enable electrostatic intercalation. Given its zwitterionic nature and multiple protonation sites, the solution pH was carefully adjusted to 8.3 using \qty{1}{\molar} \ce{NaOH}. At this specific pH, the carboxylic acid groups are fully deprotonated, and a sufficient fraction of the thiol groups are converted into their anionic thiolate form, enabling successful assembly within the LDH galleries without inducing excessive alkaline degradation of the substrate.

To verify the successful intercalation of the inhibiting anions, the loaded specimens were subjected to X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Successful incorporation of the guest anions induces a measurable shift in the characteristic low-angle $(003)$ reflection, corresponding to an expansion of the basal spacing ($d_{003}$) and confirming the modification of the interlayer gallery height.

\section{Evaluation of Corrosion Performance}
\label{sec:corrosion_evaluation}

To evaluate the active corrosion protection provided by the intercalated inhibitors, the loaded LDH-coated specimens were immersed in a \qty{0.05}{\molar} \ce{NaCl} solution. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted utilising the identical experimental setup and parameters detailed in Section~\ref{sec:inhibitor_selection}, with spectra acquired at specific immersion intervals of 0, 1, 24, 48, 72, and \qty{168}{\hour}. The acquired impedance spectra were deconvoluted using the custom automated fitting framework (Section~\ref{sec:fitting_software}). The extracted charge-transfer resistance ($R_{\text{ct}}$) and double-layer capacitance ($C_{\text{dl}}$) values were tracked as a function of immersion time to quantitatively assess and compare the self-healing efficiency of the different inhibitor systems. 

Complementing the non-destructive EIS monitoring, the specimens were periodically inspected for macroscopic visual evidence of localised attack. Additionally, potentiodynamic polarisation (PDP) measurements were performed at selected intervals to provide direct kinetic insights into the specific anodic and cathodic inhibition mechanisms.

\section{Application of Polyurethane Topcoat and Evaluation on Complex Geometries}
\label{sec:topcoat}

Following the identification of the optimal inhibitor-loaded LDH system, a water-based polyurethane colloidal formulation (supplied by CIDETEC, Spain) was applied to the coated planar specimens to form a complete multilayer protection architecture. The topcoat was deposited via anodic electrodeposition. During this process, an applied electric field drives the negatively charged polyurethane colloids toward the anodically polarised substrate, where the concurrent anodic oxidation of water generates a localised pH increase at the interface, inducing particle coagulation and the formation of a continuous polymer film. 

The deposition was carried out at room temperature by applying a constant voltage of \qty{80}{\volt} for \qty{1}{\minute}. Following deposition, the specimens were air-dried for \qty{30}{\minute} and subsequently thermally cured in an oven at \qty{120}{\degreeCelsius} for \qty{1}{\hour}. 

Although standardised quantitative adhesion tests, such as ISO 2409 (cross-hatch test) and ISO 4624:2023 (pull-off test), represent the established industrial protocols for evaluating coating adhesion performance, these could not be applied in the present study. This limitation was due to the restricted dimensions of the prepared substrates, which were insufficient to accommodate the required test geometries and dolly sizes.

Consequently, to assess the microstructural integrity and interfacial adhesion of the complete multilayer system, cross-sections of the coated planar specimens were prepared and analysed using SEM-EDS. Elemental mapping was specifically employed to evaluate the spatial distribution of key elements and to determine the degree of interpenetration between the organic polyurethane topcoat and the underlying inorganic LDH conversion layer. Furthermore, the dry film thickness of the cured topcoat was measured using an Elcometer gauge. While the electrodeposition process was also applied to complex-geometry components to demonstrate its practical versatility, all quantitative microstructural and thickness characterisations were exclusively performed on the planar specimens to ensure controlled, high-resolution analysis.

The extracted parameters confirm the trends identified from the qualitative inspection of the spectra. MBT, \ce{NaVO3}, and L-cysteine all attained $R_{\text{ct}}$ values in excess of \qty{100}{\kilo\ohm}, substantially higher than those of the remaining systems. The progressive increase in $R_{\text{ct}}$ with immersion time for these three inhibitors further supports the hypothesis of active and durable film formation at the electrode surface. Sodium molybdate, by contrast, performed comparably to the blank \ce{NaCl} solution throughout the entire immersion period, with $R_{\text{ct}}$ values consistently in the range of \qtyrange{3}{4}{\kilo\ohm}. This near-complete absence of inhibition under open-circuit conditions is consistent with the known mechanism of molybdate, which requires anodic polarisation to form a protective surface film and therefore provides negligible protection in the context of free immersion in dilute \ce{NaCl} \cite{Sastri2007, Vargel2020}. BTA and 8-HQ exhibited intermediate behaviour, with $R_{\text{ct}}$ values ranging from \qty{10}{\kilo\ohm} to \qty{30}{\kilo\ohm}, significantly lower than the three best-performing inhibitors.

The double-layer capacitance data further refine these conclusions. Sodium molybdate again showed behaviour closely resembling that of the blank \ce{NaCl} system, with $C_{\text{dl}}$ values approaching \qty{100}{\micro\farad}, consistent with an essentially unprotected electrode surface. Although BTA and 8-HQ displayed relatively low initial $C_{\text{dl}}$ values---suggesting that some degree of initial adsorption does occur---these increased rapidly with immersion time, eventually converging towards values comparable to the uninhibited system. This behaviour indicates that while both inhibitors adsorb at the electrode surface in the early stages of immersion, they fail to maintain a stable protective film over time. The progressive desorption or displacement of the inhibitor allows water molecules and electrolyte ions to repopulate the double layer; given the substantially higher dielectric constant of water ($\varepsilon_r \approx 80$) relative to that of adsorbed organic species, this ingress drives $C_{\text{dl}}$ towards values characteristic of an unprotected surface \cite{Orazem2008, Sastri2007}. In contrast, MBT, L-cysteine, and \ce{NaVO3} maintained $C_{\text{dl}}$ values on the order of \qty{10}{\micro\farad} throughout the experiment, consistent with stable inhibitor adsorption, the sustained exclusion of water from the double layer region, and a durable reduction in interfacial reactivity.

The specimen immersed in the blank \ce{NaCl} solution exhibits extensive, generalised surface degradation, serving as a reference for the uninhibited corrosion behaviour of the alloy. These are attributable to the precipitation of vanadate oxides over incipient pit sites, indicative of active inhibitor release and localised repassivation. L-cysteine performs comparably, exhibiting no discrete pits but a diffuse whitish surface appearance characteristic of mild, generalised aluminium matrix oxidation, suggesting that while localised attack is suppressed, uniform surface dissolution is not entirely prevented.

BTA and 8-HQ display intermediate levels of degradation, consistent with their moderate $R_{\text{ct}}$ values. The BTA specimen shows both discrete pitting and areas of pronounced generalised corrosion. The 8-HQ specimen similarly exhibits generalised surface corrosion alongside multiple large green-yellowish deposits. These deposits are attributable to copper-8-HQ coordination complexes formed through the interaction of the 8-HQ ligand with dissolved metal ions at active pit sites, indicating that significant localised corrosion had occurred beneath these deposits despite some degree of inhibitor activity.

Sodium molybdate presents the most severely degraded surface among the inhibited specimens, with an appearance closely resembling that of the control sample and exhibiting heavy generalised corrosion. This is fully consistent with the electrochemical data, which showed molybdate to provide essentially no inhibition under free immersion conditions in dilute \ce{NaCl}.

Based on these results, MBT, \ce{NaVO3}, and L-cysteine were identified as the best-performing inhibitors among those evaluated and were therefore selected for subsequent incorporation into the ZnAl--LDH coating system.

The EDS elemental maps of the mechanically polished reference specimens (Figure~\ref{fig:sem_eds_I}) reveal the unaltered, heterogeneous microstructure of the AC46000 alloy. A significant population of Fe--Mn-rich intermetallic particles (IMPs) is visible, consistent with the \ce{Al15(Fe,Mn)3Si2} and related phases identified in the literature. Additionally, dispersed Cu-rich and eutectic Si particles are clearly delineated. The alloying elements Mg and Zn exhibit a diffuse distribution across the aluminium matrix without preferential localisation.

Following the simplified chemical pre-treatment (Figure~\ref{fig:sem_eds_II}), a partial reduction in IMP density is observed. The larger Fe--Mn-rich particles are largely detached from the surface, leaving only scattered residual traces of these elements. Copper-rich phases exhibit partial removal, while the eutectic silicon particles persist largely unaffected, consistent with the inability of nitric acid to dissolve silicon. The distribution of Mg and Zn remains comparable to the polished reference, and the aluminium matrix appears relatively intact, indicating that the mild alkaline etching and acid desmutting steps induce only minimal bulk matrix dissolution.

In contrast, the industrial pre-treatment (Figure~\ref{fig:sem_eds_III}) induces a markedly more aggressive surface modification. Extensive dissolution of the aluminium matrix is evident, accompanied by the effective and near-complete removal of both Fe--Mn and Cu-rich intermetallic particles. This is attributed to the prolonged alkaline etching, which drives cathodic undercutting, followed by the highly oxidative acid desmutting step. Despite this aggressive attack, the eutectic silicon particles remain prominently exposed on the surface, indicating that the desmutting duration was insufficient to fully dissolve the larger Si phases, while the diffuse matrix distribution of Mg and Zn is largely retained.

Based on these microstructural observations, the industrially pre-treated specimens are hypothesised to provide the most favourable surface conditions for uniform LDH nucleation. The near-complete removal of the highly cathodic Fe--Mn and Cu-rich IMPs eliminates the primary sites of localised micro-galvanic activity that drive heterogeneous, ``flower-like'' LDH precipitation and severe matrix trenching (Section~\ref{sec:imp_ldh}). While the residual eutectic silicon particles do not trigger intense localised trenching due to their electrochemical inertness, they remain as microstructural discontinuities. Nevertheless, from a corrosion standpoint, the drastic reduction in the density of active cathodic IMPs is anticipated to severely suppress localised pitting. The chemically pre-treated specimens are expected to yield intermediate results, outperforming the polished reference by reducing the density of active IMPs, albeit to a lesser extent than the industrial protocol due to the incomplete removal of the Fe--Mn and Cu phases.

\section{LDH Synthesis}
\label{sec:results_LDH_synthesis}

Additionally, LDH growth was assessed on specimens subjected to an anodising procedure prior to synthesis, resulting in a total of four substrate conditions being evaluated. The inclusion of the anodised condition was motivated by prior work on the same alloy, which suggested that a porous anodic oxide layer could promote LDH nucleation. The XRD patterns are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:xrd_ldh}.

The XRD patterns confirm successful LDH formation on all four substrate conditions. Reference patterns for the aluminium matrix and silicon eutectic phases are included for comparison. The ZnAl--LDH phase adopts a three-layer rhombohedral stacking sequence (polytype 3R, space group $R\bar{3}m$), which gives rise to the characteristic (003) and (006) basal reflections at approximately $2\theta \approx 10^\circ$ and $2\theta \approx 20^\circ$, respectively \cite{Zhou2017}. Crucially, the anodising procedure confers no measurable advantage in terms of LDH yield. Since the bulk AC46000 alloy contains only trace amounts of Zn, this substantial and uniform Zn signal is derived exclusively from the synthesis solution. Its uniform distribution across both substrate types provides strong, direct evidence that the LDH was deposited with comparable density over both the aluminium matrix and the silicon particles.

Further insight is obtained from the Zn:Al atomic ratios. Over the aluminium matrix, the measured Zn:Al ratio is approximately 0.32; however, in this region, the Al signal contains significant contributions from both the LDH film and the underlying aluminium substrate. \section{Inhibitor Intercalation}
\label{sec:results_inhibitor_intercalation}

Following the identification of the optimal pre-treatment conditions, the three top-performing inhibitors from the pre-selection study (Section~\ref{sec:results_inhibitor_selection})---\ce{NaVO3}, MBT, and L-cysteine---were considered for intercalation into the ZnAl--LDH interlayer galleries. However, L-cysteine was excluded from the intercalation trials on the AC46000 substrates. Simultaneous parallel studies conducted on AA2024 alloys using an identical LDH synthesis protocol demonstrated that the attempted intercalation of L-cysteine induces the complete dissolution of the LDH crystalline structure. 

Because both L-cysteine and MBT contain a thiol functional group, it was anticipated that MBT might present a similar challenge. Nevertheless, the intercalation of MBT was still attempted on the AC46000 substrates due to its excellent inhibition efficiency in the pre-selection phase and the novelty of its application, as its interaction with ZnAl--LDH on this specific alloy had not been previously explored. XRD analysis was subsequently conducted to verify successful intercalation, which would be evidenced by a shift in the characteristic $2\theta$ reflection angles relative to the nitrate-form LDH. The XRD patterns before and after intercalation, as well as after immersion in \qty{0.05}{\molar} \ce{NaCl}, are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:xrd_intercalation}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/XRD_intercalation.pdf} 
    \caption{XRD patterns of LDH films in the nitrate form (control), following \ce{NaVO3} intercalation, and following the MBT intercalation attempt, each presented before and after immersion in \qty{0.05}{\molar} \ce{NaCl}.}
    \label{fig:xrd_intercalation}
\end{figure}

For the \ce{NaVO3}-intercalated system, a systematic shift in the (003) reflection is observed across the three stages. The nitrate-form LDH (control) exhibits a (003) reflection at $2\theta = 9.86^\circ$ (basal spacing \qty{8.97}{\angstrom}, gallery height \qty{4.20}{\angstrom}). Following vanadate intercalation, this shifts to $2\theta = 9.51^\circ$ (\qty{9.30}{\angstrom} spacing, \qty{4.53}{\angstrom} gallery). This expansion confirms the successful accommodation of vanadate species, reflecting the larger effective size of the intercalated anion, though the precise speciation (\ce{VO3^-}, \ce{HVO4^{2-}}, \ce{V2O7^{4-}}, etc.) was not independently resolved.

After \qty{168}{\hour} of immersion, the (003) reflection shifts to $2\theta = 10.35^\circ$ (\qty{8.55}{\angstrom} spacing, \qty{3.78}{\angstrom} gallery). This contraction indicates the progressive replacement of larger vanadate anions by smaller chloride ions (\ce{Cl^-}, radius $\approx$ \qty{1.81}{\angstrom}) via anion exchange. This definitively confirms that the LDH film actively participates in ion exchange under service conditions, releasing the inhibitor in exchange for aggressive chloride species---precisely the active, self-healing mechanism for which the system was designed. 

Alongside this shift, a progressive broadening and attenuation of the (003) reflection indicates a transition from a well-crystallised LDH structure towards a more amorphous phase. This structural degradation is attributed to the partial disruption of interlayer ordering during repeated anion exchange, and the gradual chemical attack by the corrosive environment. The latter includes locally acidic conditions from pit formation and direct chloride interaction with the hydroxide layers, progressively undermining the film's long-range crystalline order. 

Consequently, the higher-order (006) reflection becomes difficult to distinguish from background noise. While a broad feature around $2\theta \approx 20^\circ$--$22^\circ$ may correspond to this diminished (006) reflection, a distinct peak near $2\theta \approx 21^\circ$ is also present in the control sample and originates from intermetallic particles. Based on the alloy composition and persistence after etching, the most probable assignment is the $\beta$-\ce{Al4.5FeSi} phase, known for its stability during pre-treatments. Alternatively, \ce{Al2Cu} intermetallics cannot be completely ruled out, though they appear less likely as Cu-rich phases are generally more susceptible to alkaline dissolution. The $\beta$-\ce{Al4.5FeSi} assignment is consistent with EDS results showing Fe and Si retention.

In stark contrast, the XRD pattern for the MBT-intercalated specimen revealed a complete absence of crystalline diffraction peaks (Figure~\ref{fig:xrd_intercalation}). Rather than successful intercalation, exposure to the MBT solution induced the collapse of the LDH structure, mirroring the dissolution previously observed for L-cysteine. This degradation is fundamentally attributed to the deprotonated thiolate anions ($\text{-S}^-$) present in the alkaline solutions. These groups exhibit an exceptionally strong coordination affinity for \ce{Zn^{2+}} ions, acting as powerful chelating agents that actively extract zinc from the host brucite layers to form soluble zinc--thiolate complexes. This aggressive zinc leaching destabilises and collapses the LDH crystal lattice, preventing structured intercalation.

Given the consistent structural failure observed for L-cysteine in parallel studies, it was excluded from further evaluation. MBT, however, was retained for corrosion testing. In the absence of a crystalline LDH reservoir, its protective mechanism shifts fundamentally. Rather than relying on controlled release via anion exchange, the system depends on the passive barrier properties of the remnant amorphous oxide film---composed principally of aluminium and zinc oxides---combined with the direct chemisorption of MBT molecules onto the exposed alloy surface.


\section{Corrosion Performance Analysis}
\label{sec:results_corrosion_performance}

The corrosion performance of the developed LDH-based coatings was evaluated through an iterative approach comprising two distinct experimental batches. The initial phase (LDH-1) evaluated the vanadate-intercalated system synthesized under the industrial pre-treatment conditions, which ultimately underperformed relative to the uncoated alloy. Driven by these limitations, the synthesis protocol was strategically revised. The subsequent phase (LDH-2) evaluated the impact of a milder chemical pre-treatment and extended synthesis duration, comparing the performance of MBT-intercalated and \ce{NaVO3}-intercalated systems. The following sections detail the electrochemical and macroscopic degradation trends observed across both batches, highlighting the mechanistic differences in their corrosion protection capabilities and the critical influence of the underlying substrate microstructure.

The control specimen displays the greatest number of pits, though these remain relatively small and discrete. Nonetheless, there is limited but discernible evidence of vanadate inhibitor activity in specific coated specimens (Figures~\ref{fig:optical_ldh1_after_1} and \ref{fig:optical_ldh1_after_3}), which display small yellowish deposits characteristic of vanadium oxide precipitation at incipient pit sites---a hallmark of localised \ce{NaVO3} release and repassivation attempts. The presence of these deposits, despite being insufficient to prevent macroscopic corrosion, supports the conclusion that the inhibitor loading achievable within the LDH films grown under the industrial pre-treatment conditions was inadequate to suppress the corrosive activity of the newly exposed cathodic intermetallic sites.

This counterintuitive behaviour is attributed to the combined effect of the industrial pre-treatment and the subsequent LDH synthesis. The aggressive industrial etching induces severe cathodic undercutting, effectively removing superficial intermetallic particles but simultaneously digging deep trenches into the surrounding aluminium matrix. Consequently, the uncoated control specimen benefits from a relatively inert surface, as the most active cathodic sites have been physically removed. However, the subsequent LDH synthesis requires partial dissolution of the aluminium matrix to supply the requisite \ce{Al^{3+}} cations. When applied to an already deeply trenched surface, even a minor degree of matrix dissolution during the \qty{1}{\hour} synthesis is sufficient to fully expose subsurface intermetallic particles that were previously undermined and buried just below the surface. These newly exposed, highly cathodic sites then initiate severe localised pitting corrosion.

Once a pit is initiated, the geometry of the cavity further exacerbates the corrosion process. Within the confined volume of a pit, mass transport is severely restricted, preventing local dissolution products from diffusing away and hindering the replenishment of inhibitor species from the bulk electrolyte. This mass-transport limitation drives the local chemistry towards increasingly aggressive conditions (lower pH and higher chloride concentration), promoting the autocatalytic and largely uncontrolled dissolution of the aluminium matrix. Furthermore, the geometric inaccessibility of the pit interior significantly limits the ability of the vanadate inhibitor to reach and passivate the active pitting sites within the cavity. Unlike surface-exposed intermetallic particles, where inhibitor species released from the LDH film can readily diffuse to the reactive site, the restricted geometry of a deep pit severely impedes inhibitor transport. Consequently, the \ce{NaVO3} loading achievable within the LDH films is rendered insufficient to suppress cathodic activity at these buried sites. This critical assessment motivated the revision of the synthesis protocol for the second batch (LDH-2).

Furthermore, the LDH growth duration was extended to \qty{3}{\hour} to increase film density and maximise the inhibitor loading within the interlayer galleries. Both \ce{NaVO3} and MBT were evaluated as active agents, with MBT included based on its excellent inhibition efficiency demonstrated during the bare-metal pre-selection study (Section~\ref{sec:results_inhibitor_selection}).

Prior to immersion, the \ce{NaVO3}-intercalated specimens (Figures~\ref{fig:optical_ldh2_before_3} and~\ref{fig:optical_ldh2_before_4}) display visible surface heterogeneities, including areas of incomplete substrate coverage. This morphological non-uniformity partially accounts for their comparatively weaker initial electrochemical performance relative to the MBT-intercalated specimens.

At \qty{72}{\hour} of immersion, a clear differentiation between the inhibitor systems is already apparent. The MBT-intercalated specimens display a relatively homogeneous surface with no visible signs of pitting or generalised corrosion. This is in excellent agreement with the substantially lower $C_{\text{dl}}$ values recorded at this time point (\qtyrange{20}{30}{\micro\farad\per\square\centi\meter} compared to approximately \qty{70}{\micro\farad\per\square\centi\meter} for the control), indicating that the MBT-treated coating remains largely intact and that significant water uptake has not yet occurred. The \ce{NaVO3}-intercalated specimens, while not yet exhibiting discrete pits, already show a diffuse whitish surface appearance indicative of incipient generalised aluminium matrix corrosion. This visual degradation aligns with their $C_{\text{dl}}$ values, which have already converged with those of the control. The control specimen, by contrast, displays significant discrete pitting at \qty{72}{\hour}, confirming that both inhibitor-loaded systems provide measurably superior corrosion protection at intermediate immersion times.

Following \qty{168}{\hour} of immersion, the control specimen exhibits the most severe surface degradation, characterised by large discrete pits and extensive generalised matrix dissolution. The MBT-intercalated specimens present the best overall surface condition, featuring only minor pitting and a generalised yellowish surface deposit. While the precise composition of this deposit was not independently characterised, it is highly likely associated with the surface interaction products of MBT with dissolved metal species. The \ce{NaVO3}-intercalated specimens display an intermediate level of degradation---superior to the control but inferior to the MBT systems---with moderate pitting distributed across the surface alongside localised yellowish deposits. These deposits are characteristic of vanadium oxide precipitation at active pit sites, confirming that vanadate release and repassivation attempts did occur, albeit insufficiently to fully suppress corrosion progression over the full immersion period. These visual observations are fully consistent with the $R_{\text{ct}}$ and $C_{\text{dl}}$ trends and corroborate the performance ranking established by the electrochemical analysis.

The uncoated control specimen exhibits the most active corrosion potential ($E_{\text{corr}} \approx \qty{-0.60}{\volt}$) and the highest current densities across both branches. In contrast, both the MBT- and \ce{NaVO3}-intercalated specimens display a clear shift of $E_{\text{corr}}$ in the noble direction to approximately \qty{-0.52}{\volt}. Furthermore, the inhibitor-loaded systems exhibit measurably lower current densities in the anodic region; for instance, at \qty{-0.4}{\volt}, the anodic current densities of the MBT and \ce{NaVO3} specimens are approximately one order of magnitude lower than that of the control. Crucially, however, the cathodic branches of all three specimens are virtually superimposed at higher overpotentials. This overlapping cathodic behaviour confirms that neither inhibitor system effectively suppresses the cathodic oxygen reduction reaction at the intermetallic sites. 

Consequently, both \ce{NaVO3} and MBT act primarily as anodic inhibitors, retarding the dissolution of the aluminium matrix without passivating the cathodic IMPs. However, it is important to acknowledge that the PDP response reflects the cumulative behaviour of the entire coating system. The observed reduction in anodic current is therefore likely a synergistic combination of the physical barrier properties of the LDH (or remnant oxide) film and the active chemical protection provided by residual inhibitor molecules at the interface and in the local diffusion layer, making it difficult to deconvolute their individual contributions.

While the LDH-2 batch and its associated inhibitors represent a clear improvement over the bare alloy, the overall long-term corrosion protection performance remains below the ideal target level. This limitation is attributed primarily to the persistence of Fe--Mn intermetallic particles on the chemically pre-treated surface. Unlike the Cu-rich intermetallics found in wrought alloys like AA2024, which are the primary targets of vanadate and MBT inhibitors \cite{Tedim2011, Zhang2015}, the Fe--Mn IMPs characteristic of the AC46000 cast alloy are not effectively passivated by either inhibitor system. Their continued, unhindered cathodic activity sustains localised micro-galvanic corrosion that the inhibitor loading achievable within the LDH galleries is ultimately insufficient to fully suppress.

\chapter{Conclusion \& Future Work}

\section{Conclusion}

This work investigated the synthesis, optimisation, and corrosion protection performance of ZnAl--LDH conversion coatings loaded with corrosion inhibitors on the AC46000 die-cast aluminium alloy. The ultimate objective was to validate this multilayer system on geometrically complex substrates as a viable, chromate-free alternative for aerospace applications.

A pre-selection of corrosion inhibitors was first conducted by evaluating seven candidate systems via EIS over \qty{168}{\hour} of immersion in \qty{0.05}{\molar} \ce{NaCl}. MBT, \ce{NaVO3}, and L-cysteine were identified as the most effective inhibitors, demonstrating $R_{\text{ct}}$ values in excess of \qty{100}{\kilo\ohm} and stable $C_{\text{dl}}$ values on the order of \qty{10}{\micro\farad} throughout the immersion period, indicative of active and durable surface film formation. Sodium molybdate provided negligible protection, as the severe pitting of AC46000 overwhelms the redox-driven precipitation of \ce{MoO2} and \ce{MoO3} films required for surface passivation. BTA and 8-HQ exhibited intermediate and transient protection, failing to maintain stable surface films over extended immersion.

SEM-EDS characterisation of the pre-treated surfaces confirmed that the industrial pre-treatment achieved near-complete removal of Fe--Mn and Cu-rich intermetallic particles (IMPs), leaving primarily the electrochemically inert Si particles exposed. The chemical pre-treatment achieved partial removal of Fe--Mn particles, while the polished reference retained the full complement of surface IMPs. XRD analysis confirmed successful ZnAl--LDH formation on all substrate conditions, with comparable film density and basal spacing consistent with \ce{NO3^-} intercalation, regardless of the pre-treatment applied. EDS analysis of the LDH films further confirmed uniform Zn deposition across both the aluminium matrix and the Si phase. The Zn:Al ratio measured over the Si particles provided a more faithful estimate of the intrinsic LDH stoichiometry, which was broadly consistent with the theoretical ZnAl--LDH composition.

XRD analysis of the intercalated systems confirmed the successful incorporation of \ce{NaVO3}, evidenced by a systematic shift in the (003) basal reflection from $2\theta = 9.86^\circ$ to $9.51^\circ$, corresponding to an expansion of the interlayer gallery height from \qty{4.20}{\angstrom} to \qty{4.53}{\angstrom}. Following immersion, a further shift to $2\theta = 10.35^\circ$ confirmed the exchange of vanadate for chloride anions under service conditions, definitively demonstrating active inhibitor release. In contrast, the attempted intercalation of the thiol-containing inhibitors (MBT and L-cysteine) resulted in the dissolution of the LDH crystalline structure, attributed to the strong chelating affinity of their deprotonated thiolate groups for \ce{Zn^{2+}} ions in the host layers. Consequently, these systems lack a structured reservoir for controlled release, limiting their long-term active protection capabilities and restricting their efficacy to the initial stages of immersion.

Corrosion performance evaluation was conducted in two distinct batches. The first batch (LDH-1), based on the industrial pre-treatment with a \qty{1}{\hour} LDH growth time, yielded unsatisfactory corrosion protection. The uncoated control specimen outperformed all vanadate-intercalated LDH specimens, a counterintuitive result attributed to the synergistic effect of aggressive industrial etching and subsequent matrix dissolution, which exposed subsurface intermetallic particles and initiated severe pitting that the inhibitor loading was insufficient to suppress. 

The second batch (LDH-2), employing the milder chemical pre-treatment with an extended \qty{3}{\hour} LDH growth time, demonstrated a clear improvement. MBT-intercalated specimens exhibited the best corrosion protection, with a low-frequency impedance modulus approximately one order of magnitude higher than the control at \qty{1}{\hour} and substantially elevated $R_{\text{ct}}$ values at early immersion times. \ce{NaVO3}-intercalated specimens showed intermediate performance, with localised vanadate oxide deposits confirming active inhibitor release. PDP analysis confirmed that neither inhibitor system effectively suppressed the cathodic activity at Fe--Mn intermetallic sites; however, both significantly reduced the anodic dissolution rate of the aluminium matrix. Ultimately, the long-term corrosion protection performance of the LDH-2 system remains below the ideal target level, primarily due to the persistence of Fe--Mn intermetallic particles on the chemically pre-treated surface, which are not effectively passivated by either \ce{NaVO3} or MBT under the conditions investigated.

Finally, a water-based polyurethane topcoat was successfully applied to both planar and complex-geometry specimens via anodic electrodeposition. Optical inspection confirmed uniform and continuous film formation across all substrate geometries. Furthermore, the complete absence of delamination observed during the metallographic preparation of the cross-sections for SEM-EDS analysis demonstrated robust interfacial adhesion between the polyurethane topcoat and the underlying LDH conversion coating. Despite systematic thickness differences between systems (\qty{16.47 \pm 0.55}{\micro\meter} vs. \qty{26.48 \pm 1.08}{\micro\meter} for \ce{NaVO3}), uniform coverage was confirmed across all geometries. Ultimately, the successful conformal deposition of the polyurethane topcoat over the optimised LDH pre-treatment demonstrates that this environmentally benign, chromate-free multilayer architecture is not only electrochemically active, but also industrially viable for the corrosion protection of next-generation aerospace cast components.


\section{Future Work}

Several directions are identified for future investigation. The primary limitation is the inability of \ce{NaVO3} and MBT to suppress Fe--Mn intermetallic activity; future work should investigate alternative inhibitor systems such as cerium-based compounds or benzimidazole derivatives, either as standalone intercalants or in synergistic combinations. The dissolution of ZnAl--LDH by thiol-containing inhibitors also suggests exploring alternative host compositions (e.g., MgAl--LDH) or surface adsorption strategies.

The trade-off between IMP removal efficiency and subsurface exposure requires investigation of milder pre-treatment formulations and optimisation of LDH synthesis parameters (temperature, time, concentration, pH) to maximise inhibitor loading while minimising substrate attack. Long-term durability assessment via accelerated corrosion testing (neutral salt spray, cyclic testing) combined with localised electrochemical characterisation (SVET, SECM) would provide comprehensive performance validation and mechanistic insights.

Finally, comprehensive mechanical testing (pull-off adhesion, flexibility, impact resistance) and direct benchmarking against established reference coatings (TCP conversion coatings, chromate primers) under identical test conditions are required to fully validate the industrial applicability and competitiveness of the developed multilayer system.
